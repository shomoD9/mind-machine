# Why tools lose them

## Gist

Productivity tools fail to hold a scattered ambitious person when the cost of keeping the system current—visibility, integration, processing, and honest upkeep—outruns the benefit, especially as project load scales. The research treats this as a maintenance-and-fit problem more than a motivation problem: people often abandon tools that are out of sight, poorly connected to where work actually happens, or built for stable attention and linear time.

---

## Cross-cutting evidence

### Abandonment has named causes in PIM research

Jones, Bruce, Klasnja, and Jones (2008) interviewed 22 people about project-related personal information management. Fourteen reported abandoning at least one strategy; 33 abandonment episodes covered calendars, to-do lists, filing systems, email practices, and planning software. Five factors recurred:

1. **Visibility** — if the system is not peripherally present, people forget it exists (e.g., Outlook task pad not constantly open; calendar not checked daily).
2. **Integration** — systems that do not connect to where work already happens fall out of use; integration can restore visibility.
3. **Co-adoption** — shared systems fail when others do not participate.
4. **Scalability** — systems that worked for one project or life stage break when complexity grows (e.g., one notebook for personal life vs. entangled work and life; Microsoft Project with four to six concurrent timelines).
5. **Return on investment** — people abandon systems whose upkeep exceeds perceived payoff ("almost more maintenance… than it does to do it").

Source: Jones, E. A., Bruce, H., Klasnja, P. V., & Jones, W. (2008). *I give up! Five factors that contribute to the abandonment of information management strategies.* Proceedings of ASIST 2008. https://doi.org/10.1002/meet.2008.14504503115

### Task management proficiency is real; maintenance is the tax

Bellotti et al. (2004) tracked busy professionals for a month. Contrary to popular belief, participants were not poor prioritizers: 81% of tracked tasks completed, only 3% dropped without good reason. The surprise was the **effort required to stay that proficient** across many media and representations.

Source: Bellotti, V., Dalal, B., Good, N., Flynn, P., Bobrow, D. G., & Ducheneaut, N. (2004). *What a to-do: studies of task management towards the design of a personal task list manager.* CHI 2004. https://doi.org/10.1145/985692.985785

Haraty, McGrenere, and Tang (2016) found dedicated electronic task managers have **high abandonment rates** (citing informal community observation, not a controlled study). Half of DIYers had tried dedicated tools and reverted. People change tools when needs shift, needs go unmet, or a better opportunity appears. Maintenance behavior—crossing off, archiving, deleting, reorganizing—varies by person and is itself work.

Source: Haraty, M., McGrenere, J., & Tang, C. (2016). *How personal task management differs across individuals.* International Journal of Human-Computer Studies, 88, 13–37. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijhcs.2015.11.006

### External memory works; offloading still has failure modes

Gilbert (2022) reviews *intention offloading*: calendars, reminders, and placed objects can fulfill delayed intentions, guided by metacognitive beliefs about forgetting. Offloading is effective but not automatic—people have systematic biases in when they choose external vs. internal memory.

Source: Gilbert, S. J. (2022). *Outsourcing memory to external tools: A review of "intention offloading".* Psychonomic Bulletin & Review. https://doi.org/10.3758/s13423-022-02139-4

Sellen and Whittaker (2010) argue capture systems should provide **retrieval cues**, not pretend to be memory; **selectivity** beats total capture; systems must work in **synergy** with human memory rather than replace it.

Source: Sellen, A. J., & Whittaker, S. (2010). *Beyond total capture.* Communications of the ACM, 53(5), 70–77. https://doi.org/10.1145/1735223.1735243

### Tools feel less effective when attention and time are variable

Desrochers et al. (2019) surveyed adults with and without ADHD. Both groups used to-do lists, calendars, planners, and timers; ADHD respondents rated their strategies **less effective** despite similar tool mix. None rated strategies "extremely effective."

Source: Desrochers, B., et al. (2019). *Evaluation of why individuals with ADHD struggle to find effective digital time management tools.* ACM ASSETS 2019. https://doi.org/10.1145/3308561.3354622

Qualitative work with adults with ADHD (Campbell et al., cited in Hussein et al., 2026) reports productivity tools built on stable attention and linear time; participants described elaborate planning in isolation becoming **avoidance** ("I sometimes spend more time organizing the to-do app than doing the task").

Source: Hussein, R., et al. (2026). *"Not Just Me and My To-Do List": Understanding challenges of task management for adults with ADHD and the need for AI-augmented social scaffolds.* arXiv:2603.17258. https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.17258

*Note:* Hussein et al. cite Oliveira et al. (2025) for a feature analysis of Trello, Todoist, Focus@Will, and Forest. That Oliveira paper was not independently retrieved in this research pass; claims about those specific products below rely on Hussein et al.'s secondary summary unless otherwise noted.

## Task lists

### Evidence

- Bellotti et al. (2004): task lists are one medium among many; email is a common reminder source; completion depends on representation and context, not list hygiene alone.
- Haraty et al. (2016): list **maintenance** (organizing, archiving, deleting) is a distinct behavior; make-doers minimize overhead and may lose visibility; DIYers personalize heavily; dedicated apps often mismatch actual practice.
- Jones et al. (2008): to-do list formats and Outlook task pads abandoned for low visibility and poor ROI.
- Desrochers et al. (2019): to-do lists are widely used by ADHD adults but rated less effective.
- Hussein et al. (2026): tools emphasize completion over **task initiation**, time estimation, and emotional regulation; organizing can substitute for doing.

### Inference

For someone with many open projects, a static list becomes stale quickly: each missed review cycle widens the gap between the list and lived priorities. The tool does not fail because they cannot prioritize; it fails because keeping the list honest costs continuous executive work across every active thread.

### Folklore (not evidenced here)

- "Week three" abandonment of planners and ADHD apps — widely repeated in ADHD blogs and marketing copy; no peer-reviewed schedule found.
- "Broken streaks cause permanent abandonment" — plausible design critique, not a sourced finding in the papers above.

## Second brains (personal knowledge management)

### Evidence

- Forte (2023), first-party BASB methodology: a second brain requires ongoing **CODE** — Capture, Organize, Distill, Express — plus progressive summarization and periodic review so notes stay actionable. Forte explicitly warns against haphazard capture with no follow-through.
- Sellen & Whittaker (2010): unfocused "capture everything" is the wrong design target; value lies in curated cues for retrieval.
- Jones et al. (2008): filing, tagging, nomenclature, and literature-tracking systems abandoned when scalability or ROI collapsed.

Source: Forte, T. (2023). *Building a Second Brain: The definitive introductory guide.* Forte Labs. https://fortelabs.com/blog/basboverview/

### Inference

Second-brain systems ask for capture **and** curation **and** distillation **and** output. For a mind that switches contexts often, the Organize and Distill steps become a second job. Notes accumulate faster than processing; the archive stops cueing action and becomes another place intentions go to die.

### Folklore

- "You must use Obsidian/Notion/Evernote" — product preference, not research finding.
- BASB's own marketing claims ("proven process") are first-party positioning, not independent validation.

## Calendars

### Evidence

- Jones et al. (2008): calendaring abandoned when not checked daily; visibility and integration with email matter (Google Calendar adoption improved when "add to calendar" appeared in email flow).
- Haraty et al. (2016), citing Payne (1993): mismatch between how people model time and how calendar software models time; design guidelines include making "today" perceptually distinct.
- Hussein et al. (2026), via Desrochers et al.: calendars are heavily used but perceived as less effective by ADHD adults.

Source: Payne, S. J. (1993). *Understanding calendar use.* Human-Computer Interaction, 8(2), 83–100. (cited in Haraty et al., 2016)

### Inference

Calendars assume you will (a) enter intentions at capture time, (b) estimate duration accurately, and (c) look at the grid daily. A scattered ambitious person generates intentions faster than they calendar them; stale blocks and empty gaps erode trust. The calendar becomes a record of optimistic past selves, not a guide to the next hour.

### Folklore

- "Calendar bankruptcy" as a named practice — organizational journalism (Perlow, Hadley, & Eun), not abandonment research.

## Focus timers

### Evidence

- Cirillo (official Pomodoro site): the technique is **not just a timer**; it includes Activity Inventory, To-Do Today, Records sheets, daily and weekly processes, interruption logging, and effort estimation.
- Biwer et al. (2023): Pomodoro-style systematic breaks can reduce perceived difficulty vs. self-regulated breaks in lab study conditions.
- van Hooijdonk et al. (2025): in a 2-hour study session, Pomodoro breaks led to **faster increase in fatigue** and **faster decrease in motivation** vs. self-regulated breaks; no overall productivity difference.
- Alshardan et al. (2026): neurodivergent students adapt timer-based blockers (including **Forest**) as **initiation cues**, not rigid work intervals; fixed schedules clash with non-linear rhythms and poor time estimation.

Sources:
- Cirillo, F. *Pomodoro® Technique.* https://www.pomodorotechnique.com/
- Biwer, F., et al. (2023). *Understanding effort regulation: Comparing "Pomodoro" breaks and self-regulated breaks.* British Journal of Educational Psychology. https://doi.org/10.1111/bjep.12593
- van Hooijdonk, I., et al. (2025). *Investigating the effectiveness of self-regulated, Pomodoro, and Flowtime break-taking techniques among students.* Behavioral Sciences, 15(7), 861. https://doi.org/10.3390/bs15070861
- Alshardan, A., et al. (2026). *Advancing inclusive digital well-being tools: How neurodivergent students use distraction blockers.* CHI 2026. https://doi.org/10.1145/3772318.3790801

### Inference

A timer alone does not choose what to work on, restart after interruption, or forgive a blown session. When hyperfocus and avoidance alternate, rigid 25-minute boxes fight the actual rhythm. The surrounding Pomodoro paperwork (inventory, records, daily planning) reintroduces the same maintenance tax as task lists.

### Folklore

- "Pomodoro fixes procrastination" — mixed small studies; Dizon et al. and Almalki et al. show limited or non-significant effects in some populations (summarized in secondary reviews, not re-verified here).

## Body doubling

### Evidence

- Ara et al. (2025): body doubling is community-popular but **prior controlled work is inconclusive**; conventional doubling needs another person's presence, which is logistically hard and can raise **social anxiety and fear of judgment**; one cited study (Born, 2024) found no significant effect on performance/focus/calm in ACT reading tasks with EEG.
- Ara et al. (2025), Study 2 (n=12, VR bricklaying): with ADHD participants, human and AI body doubles improved speed and perceived attention vs. alone — a small, task-specific positive signal.
- Eagle et al. (2024), cited in Ara et al.: survey of 220 people — self-reported benefits for initiation and companionship; experimental studies cited as showing no significant behavioral effect.
- Hussein et al. (2026): adults with ADHD need **social scaffolds** and accountability beyond solo lists; tools that operate in isolation miss situational triggers.

Sources:
- Ara, Z., et al. (2025). *You are not alone: Designing body doubling for ADHD in virtual reality.* arXiv:2509.12153. https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.12153
- Born, M., et al. (2024). *Reading between the lines: Exploring body doubling in ADHD using EEG.* (cited in Ara et al., 2025)
- Eagle, E., et al. (2024). (cited in Ara et al., 2025)

### Inference

Body doubling helps most at **initiation** and **companionship**, not at holding intentions across days. It is session-bound: you still need a task chosen, a time protected, and a way back after dropout. Scheduling friction, social discomfort, and inconclusive efficacy make it easy to try once and not return — especially when project switching means constantly re-negotiating what to double on.

### Folklore

- FLOWN's 12-week observational report (company blog, no control group) — marketing/observational, not causal evidence.

## Capture inboxes

### Evidence

- Allen / David Allen Company (2011, 2023): GTD capture requires **every open loop** in a trusted collection tool, **as few inboxes as possible**, and **processing to empty regularly** — deciding what each item means and not putting it back in "in."
- Whittaker & Sidner (1996): email inboxes become overloaded task-and-archive hybrids; filing attempts often fail; important items get lost in archives.
- Fisher, Brush, Gleave, & Smith (2011): complex folder hierarchies for email refinding are **inefficient** and do not improve retrieval success vs. search and threading.
- Sellen & Whittaker (2010): selective capture and cues, not hoarding.

Sources:
- Forrister, K. (2011). *GTD best practices: Collect (Part 1 of 5).* David Allen Company. https://gettingthingsdone.com/2011/10/gtd-best-practices-collect-part-1-of-5/
- David Allen Company. *What is GTD.* https://gettingthingsdone.com/what-is-gtd/
- Whittaker, S., & Sidner, C. (1996). *Email overload: Exploring personal information management of email.* CHI 1996. https://doi.org/10.1145/238386.238530
- Fisher, D., Brush, A. J., Gleave, E., & Smith, M. A. (2011). *Am I wasting my time organizing email?* CHI 2011. https://doi.org/10.1145/1978942.1979457

### Inference

Capture inboxes succeed only while **processing keeps pace with intake**. A scattered ambitious person generates captures faster than they clarify, tag, and route them. The inbox becomes a guilt pile: visually present (unlike a forgotten calendar) but cognitively unusable because items lack decisions attached. Capture without clarify is storage, not commitment.

### Folklore

- "Inbox zero" as universal goal — practice popularized in productivity culture; Whittaker's work treats both "leave everything in inbox" and "clean regularly" as strategies people swear by and abandon.

## Synthesis: why these tools lose a scattered ambitious person

### What the evidence supports

| Mechanism | Supported by |
|-----------|----------------|
| Maintenance cost exceeds benefit | Jones et al. 2008; Bellotti et al. 2004; Haraty et al. 2016 |
| Low visibility / poor integration | Jones et al. 2008 |
| System breaks as load scales | Jones et al. 2008; Haraty et al. 2015 (changing needs) |
| Capture without processing fails | Whittaker & Sidner 1996; Allen Co. GTD; Fisher et al. 2011 |
| Mismatch with variable attention/time | Desrochers et al. 2019; Alshardan et al. 2026; Hussein et al. 2026 |
| Organizing substitutes for acting | Hussein et al. 2026 |
| Session tools don't hold cross-day intentions | Body doubling and timer literature scope |
| Tools can offload memory when maintained | Gilbert 2022; Sellen & Whittaker 2010 |

### Inference (not directly tested on "scattered ambitious" as a construct)

The scattered ambitious profile — many projects, frequent switching, weak hold on formed intentions — amplifies **scalability** and **ROI** failures. Each new thread adds capture and maintenance surface faster than review capacity. Tools built for stable attention punish lapse: outdated lists, missed blocks, and unprocessed inboxes read as personal failure rather than system mismatch. The person is not failing to "be productive"; the tool is failing to stay synchronized with a life that will not hold still.

### What we do not know from these sources

- No study labels "scattered ambitious" or tests abandonment specifically in ambitious multi-project adults without ADHD diagnosis.
- No robust evidence ranks which tool category fails first.
- Oliveira et al. (2025) platform feature analysis was not directly read; product-specific claims about Trello, Todoist, and Forest rely on Hussein et al.'s citation.
