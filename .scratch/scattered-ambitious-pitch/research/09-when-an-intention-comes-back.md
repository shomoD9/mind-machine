# When an intention comes back

## Gist

Sources treat the **moment of action** as where an intention starts governing behavior again: a cue must retrieve the intended action when the person could do it next — via focal event cues, time monitoring, implementation-intention links, or a consulted external reminder placed as a perceptual trigger. Merely capturing, storing, or resurfacing related material can leave the person with notes they still do not consult or act on; later review is voluntary retrieval, not automatic PM.

---

## How the three moments map to the literature

| Moment | What the scattered ambitious person is doing | What sources study | Governs action? |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1. While capturing** — a related past record appears beside the new thought | Parallel capture; associative surfacing | Almost no direct PM/offloading work on this layout; closest: reminder content, spontaneous retrieval during wrong phase, context reinstatement | **Usually not treated as sufficient.** Related material can activate intention fragments without changing the next act (Guynn et al., 1998; Einstein et al., 2005). |
| **2. At action** — the intention must change what they do next | Ongoing task interrupted by cue or alert | Event-based PM, time-based PM, implementation intentions, Gilbert intention-offloading triggers | **Yes — this is the causal window** in PM and offloading paradigms. |
| **3. Later review** — they go looking | Deliberate search of diary, list, inbox | Retrospective memory, cognitive offloading at test, strategic reminder-setting | **Conditional.** Works only if the person initiates search and the record links cue to action; can succeed as "having notes" without behavior change (Kelly & Risko, 2019a; Gilbert et al., 2019). |

## Event-based vs time-based prospective memory cues

**Event-based PM** ties the intended action to noticing an environmental event while doing other work (Einstein & McDaniel, 1990). Retrieval can be supported by **monitoring** (capacity-consuming search for cues) or **spontaneous retrieval** when the cue is focal — processed anyway as part of the ongoing task (McDaniel & Einstein, 2000; Einstein & McDaniel, 2005; Einstein et al., 2005). Focal cues can support good PM with little ongoing-task cost; nonfocal cues depend more on monitoring and fail more often when attention is divided (Scullin, McDaniel, Shelton, & Lee, 2010).

**Time-based PM** requires self-initiated monitoring of elapsed time — typically **clock checking** with frequency rising as the target time nears (Harris & Wilkins, 1982, cited in Park et al., 1997). Park, Hertzog, Kidder, Morrell, & Mayhorn (1997) found larger age deficits on time-based than event-based PM; older adults checked the clock less and made time-monitoring errors even when monitoring was the sole task. Event-based PM imposed a **higher concurrent cost** on an ongoing working-memory task, implying heavier attentional demand despite sometimes better raw PM scores.

**Implication for goal neglect.** Time-based intentions fail when the person stops monitoring time; event-based intentions fail when cues are nonfocal, monitoring is withdrawn under load, or the target–action association is weak. Both are **retrieval-at-the-moment** problems, not mere recall problems.

## Implementation intentions (Gollwitzer and tests)

**Core claim.** A goal intention ("I intend to achieve X") is insufficient; an **implementation intention** ("If situation Y, then I will do Z") links a anticipated cue to a specific response and **delegates control to the situation** (Gollwitzer, 1993, cited in Gollwitzer & Brandstätter, 1997).

**Empirical tests.** Gollwitzer & Brandstätter (1997): difficult goals with implementation intentions completed ~3× more often than without (22% vs 62%); Study 3 showed faster initiation when the specified opportunity appeared. Sheeran & Orbell (1999): vitamin-taking over weeks — implementation-intention group missed fewer doses; lower self-reported "forgetting" mediated the effect; participants appeared to **pass control to environmental cues** in the plan. Chasteen, Park, & Schwarz (2001): implementation intentions more than doubled self-initiated PM on a task **without salient external cues**; no benefit on a salient-cue task — if-then plans help most when the person must self-initiate, not when a focal cue already exists.

**Meta-analytic scope.** Gollwitzer & Sheeran (2006): d = 0.65 on goal attainment across 94 tests, including initiation, shielding, and disengagement. Sheeran, Listrom, & Gollwitzer (2025): 62 tests on prospective memory specifically, d = 0.40; contingent if-then format and rehearsal enlarged effects.

**Boundary.** McDaniel & Scullin (2010): implementation intentions **do not automatize** PM responding — under high attention demands, II benefits shrink; PM still requires controlled processes in demanding contexts (abstract; full text not retrieved).

**Moment mapping.** Implementation intentions are encoded **before** the action window and operate at **moment 2** when the specified cue appears. They do not describe surfacing related records during parallel capture (moment 1).

## Intention offloading (Gilbert and primary studies)

**Definition.** Gilbert (2015) studies **strategic offloading**: participants hold brief delayed intentions during an ongoing task and may place target items beside the intended edge as **perceptual triggers** — analogous to leaving an object by the door. When used, the externalized intention is **directly cued by location**; mental rehearsal is unnecessary.

**When offloading happens.** Higher memory load (1 vs 3 targets), interruptions during the retention interval, older age, lower metacognitive confidence, and lower objective unaided ability all increase reminder-setting (Gilbert, 2015; Gilbert, 2015b). Offloading is **adaptive**: conditions that impair unaided PM predict more externalizing, and more externalizing predicts better accuracy (Gilbert, 2015).

**When offloading governs action.** The external reminder works at **moment 2**: when the target circle reaches its position, its placement cues the correct drag. Setting a reminder without that consultation path does not appear in the paradigm — the trigger is inseparable from the action locus.

**Time-based offloading.** Tsai & Gilbert (2025): for press-key-at-time intentions under ongoing load, optional digital reminders improved accuracy; offloading rose with delay length and when the clock was hidden (requiring self-initiated time monitoring). Reminder use was driven partly by **metacognitive beliefs** about need, not only objective need — people may set alerts they do not optimally need, or fail to set them when they do.

**Over-offloading without consultation.** Gilbert, Bird, Carpenter, Fleming, Sachdeva, & Tsai (2019): participants were biased toward external reminders even when financially incentivized to rely on unaided memory; bias tracked erroneous underconfidence. Having set a reminder is not the same as using it wisely at the action moment.

## Whether reminders and external stores are consulted — and when they fail

**Reminders can fail entirely.** Guynn, McDaniel, & Einstein (1998), four experiments: generic reminders during a retention interval **did not** improve PM over no reminder. Reminders naming **only target events** did not help — re-exposure to cue words without the intended action did not reinstate the target–action link. Reminders naming **both target and action** did help; action-only reminders helped partially. The associative link between cue and intended behavior is what must be reactivated.

**Setting ≠ consulting.** Gilbert's offloading work separates **creating** a trigger from **fulfilling** the intention, but in that task the trigger is consulted automatically when the target arrives. In diary/smartphone use, creation is voluntary and consultation at the right moment is not guaranteed. Gilbert et al. (2019) show systematic **over-creation** of reminders relative to optimal strategy.

**External stores and "having notes."** Kelly & Risko (2019a): participants who stored all words externally across trials and **expected** access at recall encoded less internally; when access was denied at test, recall was worse than for participants who never relied on offloading. Greater reported reliance on the external store predicted worse recall when the store was unavailable. Storing information externally can produce a feeling of security ("I have it written down") while weakening the internal trace needed if the store is not consulted at the action moment.

**Notifications dismissed.** Pielot, Vradi, & Park (2018): experience-sampling study (N = 17) — users often dismiss notifications they still judge valuable; dismissal does not mean the information was integrated into behavior. (Small N; field observation, not PM lab paradigm.)

**Reminder content at action time.** For time-based PM, Tsai & Gilbert (2025): a visible clock acts as an external cue reducing need for offloading; hidden clocks increase offloading. Park et al. (1997): without clock monitoring, time-based intentions fail even when the person knows what to do.

- Guynn, M. J., McDaniel, M. A., & Einstein, G. O. (1998). Prospective memory: When reminders fail. *Memory & Cognition*, 26(2), 287–298. https://doi.org/10.3758/BF03201140 (full text excerpts retrieved)
- Kelly, M. O., & Risko, E. F. (2019). Revisiting the influence of offloading memory on free recall. *Memory & Cognition*, 47(5), 858–868. https://doi.org/10.3758/s13421-021-01237-3 (full text retrieved via Springer)
- Pielot, M., Vradi, S., & Park, S. (2018). *Dismissed!* Proceedings of the 20th International Conference on Human-Computer Interaction with Mobile Devices and Services (MobileHCI '18). https://doi.org/10.1145/3229434.3229445 (abstract; full text not retrieved)

## Resurfacing related material during ongoing work vs later review

**During ongoing work (closest to moment 1).** Einstein et al. (2005), Experiment 5 (described in Scullin, Einstein, & McDaniel, 2009): when a PM target word appears during a phase where the intention is **suspended**, it still slows responses — **spontaneous retrieval** of intention-related information without executing the action. That is activation without governance. Guynn et al. (1998) parallel: seeing target words again (target-only reminders) does not improve PM — related material resurfacing without the action link does not change behavior.

**Context reinstatement at resumption (between moments 1 and 2).** Marsh, Cook, & Hicks (2014): after interruptions, **reinstating the prevailing context** of the interrupted task alleviated PM deficits for delayed-execute intentions; cues encountered during the interrupting task were remembered worse. Dodhia & Dismukes (2009): failing to resume interrupted tasks is a PM problem — resumption cues are often **conceptual**, not a single perceptual trigger, so related material during the interrupting task may not be interpreted as "time to act."

**Later review (moment 3).** Retrospective offloading literature (Kelly & Risko, 2019a) treats retrieval as a **deliberate test-phase** behavior: success depends on choosing to consult the store and on what was encoded when saving. Gilbert (2015) naturalistic follow-up: offloading task performance weakly predicted fulfilling a real-world URL visit one week later — external strategies correlate with long-horizon fulfillment, but effect sizes are small. Later review is not automatic PM; it is search plus comprehension of what the record meant to do.

- Scullin, M. K., Einstein, G. O., & McDaniel, M. A. (2009). Evidence for spontaneous retrieval of suspended but not finished prospective memories. *Memory & Cognition*, 37(4), 425–433. https://doi.org/10.3758/MC.37.4.425 (abstract; full text not retrieved)
- Marsh, R. L., Cook, G. I., & Hicks, J. L. (2014). The role of interruptions and contextual associations in delayed-execute prospective memory. *Applied Cognitive Psychology*, 28(1), 91–99. https://doi.org/10.1002/acp.2978 (full text retrieved)
- Dodhia, R. M., & Dismukes, R. K. (2009). Interruptions create prospective memory tasks. *Applied Cognitive Psychology*, 23(6), 879–891. https://doi.org/10.1002/acp.1441 (full text retrieved)
- Kelly, M. O., & Risko, E. F. (2019), as above

## Synthesis for goal neglect

**What restores governance.** Across PM, implementation intentions, and intention offloading, the recurring condition is: at the **decision point for the next act**, something retrieves **both the cue and the intended action** — focal event, monitored time, if-then link, or a consulted external trigger placed where action happens.

**What can fail while the person still "has notes."**
- Records stored without a strong cue–action link (Guynn et al., 1998)
- External stores that reduce internal encoding and are not consulted when needed (Kelly & Risko, 2019a)
- Reminders set from metacognitive bias rather than need (Gilbert et al., 2019)
- Related material or target words resurfacing without action specification (Guynn et al., 1998; spontaneous retrieval without execution, Einstein et al., 2005)
- Later review that is optional, delayed, and decoupled from the action context (retrospective offloading; Marsh et al., 2014 on context loss after interruption)

**Moment 1 gap.** No primary PM or offloading study directly tests "related past record appears beside a new capture burst." The nearest evidence says associative surfacing alone is insufficient for behavior change unless it reinstates a target–action link at a point where the person can act.
