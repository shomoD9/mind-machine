# Goals without planning

## Gist

No body of work tests a schedule-free way for adults with ADHD to finish things. Several bodies of work cover parts of the question under different names, and none of them were tested on adult ADHD makers who publish. What the primary literature supports:

- **Cue-linked action beats clock-linked action in some ADHD memory tasks.** In children with ADHD, memory for intended actions fails more consistently when it depends on watching the clock than when an event cue is present. Adult results are mixed (three studies, three different outcomes). No study compares an event cue with a time reminder as an intervention.
- **If-then plans, the closest established mechanism to a cue-driven product, helped children with ADHD on lab tasks** (small studies, one research group). Across clinical and sub-clinical samples the pooled effect on goal attainment is d+ = 0.99. The mechanism still needs the person to write the plan in advance, and I found no adult ADHD test.
- **Goal reminders reduce goal-neglect errors in lab tasks** (non-ADHD students). Inattention symptoms predict goal neglect in community samples.
- **Reward delay is a robust ADHD correlate.** Delay discounting is higher in ADHD (d = 0.43, case-control meta-analysis) and boredom correlates with ADHD at r = 0.40. These findings fit a weak pull from distant or low-stimulation tasks. "Interest-based nervous system" does not appear as a construct in the primary literature.
- **Hyperfocus is mixed.** One large online study and one clinic study found more hyperfocus in ADHD; a third found no difference between 78 diagnosed adults and matched controls. All three used self-report.
- **The theories that endorse plan-free action** (situated action, effectuation, emergent strategy, opportunistic planning) come from non-ADHD populations (designers, entrepreneurs, cognitive modelling). Effectuation is contested.
- **Schedule-based treatment has positive trials in adults with ADHD.** CBT packages that teach time management and planning show g = 0.65 against controls in a meta-analysis (32 studies). "Planning fails people with ADHD" is untested as a general claim. The accurate statement is that planning skill is a documented weak point, and training it helps on average.
- **Finishing.** ADHD symptoms predict starting entrepreneurial ventures and worse post-launch outcomes (47 studies). Procrastination correlates with inattention and partly runs through prospective memory failure. No study measures publishing, public sharing, or finishing creative work in ADHD.
- **Evaluation fear.** Social-evaluative threat is a lab-established stressor in general populations. Evidence that it blocks publishing in ADHD does not exist. Perfectionism evidence in ADHD is thin and points both ways. Rejection sensitivity is a validated social-cognition construct (Downey & Feldman, 1996) with a small and mixed ADHD literature. "Rejection sensitive dysphoria" is a clinician-coined term outside DSM and ICD, with limited empirical support.

The final section lists the product-relevant questions that have no direct evidence.

How to read this note. Population tags: **[ADHD-adult]**, **[ADHD-child]**, **[non-ADHD]**. Each section states what the source measured. Where a popular claim differs from what was measured, the section says so. "Implication" lines are my inferences. They are not findings. Note 09 covers general prospective memory and implementation-intention evidence, so this note adds the ADHD-specific layer and does not repeat that note.

## 1. Implementation intentions in ADHD

**Claim.** An if-then plan ("If situation Y, then I do Z") hands control of an action to a situation. Gollwitzer (1999) is the origin.

**Evidence.**
- **[ADHD-child]** Gawrilow & Gollwitzer (2008, two studies): children with ADHD who added if-then plans to a suppression goal raised Go/NoGo inhibition to the level of children without ADHD (Study 1). Plans plus stimulant medication gave the best suppression (Study 2). Measured: accuracy on a Go/NoGo lab task. Sample sizes are not in the abstract and I did not retrieve the full text.
- **[ADHD-child]** Paul-Jordanov, Bechtold & Gawrilow (2010): if-then plans and methylphenidate were comparable in modulating the P300 and improving Go/NoGo inhibition. The abstract reports no sample size.
- **[ADHD-child]** Gawrilow, Gollwitzer & Oettingen (2011, two studies): fewer perseverative errors on a shifting task and better math performance under distraction when children with ADHD made if-then plans.
- **[ADHD-child, at-risk]** Gawrilow, Morgenroth, Schultz, Oettingen & Gollwitzer (2013): sixth and seventh graders at risk for ADHD learned mental contrasting with implementation intentions. Parents rated self-regulation two weeks later. Both at-risk and not-at-risk children benefited more than from a learning-style intervention, and the benefit was larger for at-risk children. Measured: parent rating, two weeks.
- **[mixed clinical/analogue]** Toli, Webb & Hardy (2016): 29 experimental studies from 18 records, people with DSM-IV/ICD-10 diagnoses or above-cutoff scores. After excluding one outlier, d+ = 0.99 (k = 28, N = 1,636) on goal attainment. The abstract does not say whether ADHD studies are included, and I did not confirm.
- **[ADHD-adult]** I found no study of implementation intentions in adults with ADHD (PubMed and OpenAlex searches).
- **[non-ADHD]** Allan, Sniehotta & Johnston (2013): in a snacking study, the plan intervention improved goal attainment in poor planners (n = 144 in Study 2) and not in skilled planners. Spontaneous plans explained more variance when skilled planners made them (Study 1, n = 72). The authors conclude that planning interventions can compensate for low planning skill.
- **[ADHD-adult, indirect]** Fuermaier et al. (2013): 45 unmedicated adults with ADHD vs 45 matched controls on a complex prospective memory paradigm. Large impairment in task planning. Negligible to small impairment in plan recall, self-initiation and execution. The authors conclude that prospective memory problems in adult ADHD mainly come from deficient planning.

**Strength.** Weak to moderate. Child lab tasks, short horizon, a small number of papers from one research group, outcomes are inhibition accuracy and parent ratings. The meta-analytic effect (d+ = 0.99) pools many conditions. A common claim is that "if-then plans work for ADHD". The measured result is better Go/NoGo and shifting performance in children, and better parent-rated self-regulation at two weeks.

**Implication (inference).** The mechanism ties action to a situation and not to a clock, which matches a no-schedule product. Two limits follow. The studies used cues the person or experimenter specified in advance, and the product would supply cues the person did not specify. The Fuermaier and Allan results suggest that authoring a plan is itself a weak point, which argues for cues generated for the person.

## 2. Event-based versus time-based cues in ADHD (question b)

**Claim.** Event-based prospective memory (a cue in the environment triggers the intended act) draws on spontaneous retrieval. Time-based prospective memory needs self-initiated clock monitoring. Executive weakness should hurt time-based tasks more. Note 09 covers the general framework (Einstein & McDaniel; McDaniel & Einstein).

**Evidence.** Laboratory and virtual-task measures of remembering, not intervention trials.

| Study | Sample | Task type | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| Kerns & Price (2001) | [ADHD-child], vs IQ-matched controls (N not in abstract) | time-based | Deficit in ADHD; unrelated to IQ |
| Zinke et al. (2010) | [ADHD-child] 22 vs 39 | time-based | Fewer correct PM responses; overall frequency and accuracy of clock monitoring did not differ; monitoring just before target times may relate to success |
| Talbot & Kerns (2014) | [ADHD-child] 69 children, 8–13 | both | ADHD worse on event-based, time-based and time reproduction; symptom severity and parent-reported PM problems related to time-based performance and not event-based |
| Mioni et al. (2017) | [ADHD-child] 23 vs matched | time-based | Less accurate and less strategic time monitoring; time perception predicted accuracy |
| Yang et al. (2019) | [ADHD-child] 28 vs 28 | event, time, activity | PM performance intact in ADHD; time-based worst in both groups; ADHD lower on the ongoing task in the event-based condition |
| Costanzo et al. (2021) | [ADHD-child] 24 vs 23 | event-based | Deficits under working-memory load and non-focal cues; accuracy in a reward condition did not differ from baseline |
| Seesjärvi et al. (2025) | [ADHD-child] 71 vs 71, 9–13 | time-based, virtual reality | Lower performance; strategic time monitoring explained 22.1% of variance and fully mediated the ADHD effect |
| Talbot, Müller & Kerns (2018) | review, 6 child studies | both | Most studies found a time-based deficit and no event-based deficit; no measures or evidence-based interventions targeting PM in children with ADHD |
| Altgassen et al. (2014) | [ADHD-adult] 25 vs 25 | both, one paradigm (Dresden Breakfast Task) | No group difference in event-based PM; large impairment in time-based PM |
| Altgassen, Koch & Kliegel (2019, online 2014) | [ADHD-adult] 18 vs 18 | event-based | Reduced PM in ADHD; added inhibitory load changed nothing |
| Fuermaier et al. (2013) | [ADHD-adult] 45 vs 45 | complex PM | Large planning deficit; negligible to small deficits in recall, self-initiation, execution |
| Altgassen, Scheres & Edel (2019) | [ADHD-adult] 29 vs 24 | lab and real-life PM | Clear deficits in everyday PM; recalled and executed fewer own real-life intentions |
| Jylkkä et al. (2023) | [ADHD-adult] 112 vs 255, online 3D game | everyday tasks from memory | ADHD showed more task-irrelevant actions; self-reported strategy use strongly predicted performance in both groups |

Intervention-adjacent results:
- **[ADHD-adult]** Nordby et al. (2022): 109 adults with self-reported ADHD, randomized weekly SMS reminders to an internet intervention. Reminders gave faster login and more time on some modules, and no effect on module completion, logins or coping-strategy practice. Measured: adherence to a self-guided course. This is a time-triggered reminder to do a course, and no event-cue arm existed.
- **[ADHD-adult]** Altgassen, Heinrich & Edel (2026): episodic future thinking at encoding improved everyday intention execution across 63 adults with ADHD and 64 controls. The effect was no longer significant after controlling for verbal ability, and there was no group-by-condition interaction.

**Strength.** Child evidence leans toward more consistent time-based than event-based deficits (review of 6 studies, plus several later studies). Adult evidence is three studies with three profiles. Event-based deficits appear under working-memory load, non-focal cues, and in some adult samples. The common claim "ADHD brains need external cues because they cannot track time" goes beyond what was measured. The studies measured performance on prospective memory tasks. No study compared event cues and time reminders as interventions, and none tested cue effectiveness over days or weeks. Time perception deficits are well documented in ADHD (Noreika, Falter & Rubia, 2013, review, "most consistent" impairments in duration discrimination, reproduction, sensorimotor synchronization and delay discounting), and strategic time monitoring mediates the time-based deficit in one child sample (Seesjärvi et al.).

**Answer to (b).** The evidence does not establish that event-based cues are more effective than time-based reminders for people with ADHD. It shows that time-based remembering is the more consistent weak spot in children, and that the adult picture is mixed.

**Implication (inference).** A product that relies on events (opening a text box, scrolling) avoids the one task type with the most consistent deficit. Event-based performance still dropped under load and with non-focal cues, so the cue's position in the user's focus matters.

## 3. Goal neglect, goal reminders, goal systems, goal shielding

**Claim.** Goal neglect is failing to act on a task rule or goal that the person understands (Duncan et al., 1996; Bhandari & Duncan, 2013 describe it this way). Goal shielding: an active focal goal inhibits alternative goals (Shah, Friedman & Kruglanski, 2002). Goal Management Training was built from the goal-neglect account (Levine et al., 2000).

**Evidence.**
- **[non-ADHD]** Hood & Hutchison (2021): 212 university participants, Stroop task. Periodic goal reminders (vocalizing the task goal every 12 trials) removed the relationship between working-memory capacity and Stroop errors; a rehearsed non-goal statement did not. Hood, Charbonneau & Hutchison (2022): the between-subjects manipulation showed a strong reminder benefit that lasted at least 24 trials and accumulated; the within-subject manipulation showed no consistent benefit over non-goal statements. Measured: lab Stroop errors over minutes.
- **[community, inattention symptoms]** Elisa, Balaguer-Ballester & Parris (2016): adults 18–35 rated on the Conners Adult ADHD scale. High inattention uniquely predicted performance on a goal-neglect task (Experiment 2), which the authors call the first reported association between inattention and goal neglect. Arabacı & Parris (2020): inattentive traits (and not hyperactive/impulsive traits) predicted switch costs when switching was predictable and working-memory load was high; the mechanism was a tendency to neglect preparatory proactive control. Sample sizes are not in the abstracts. These are trait-based community samples and not diagnosed samples.
- **[TBI]** Levine et al. (2000): Goal Management Training improved everyday paper-and-pencil tasks in a randomized TBI study and a meal-preparation case.
- **[ADHD-adult]** Goal Management Training studies: In de Braek et al. (2017), 12 vs 15 adults, clinician-blind interview better in the intervention group (GMT combined with psychoeducation and counseling). Jensen et al. (2021), open trial, 21 adults, improved inhibition at post-test and 6 months, and reduced self-reported symptoms at 6 months. Hanssen et al. (2023), randomized, 81 adults: everyday executive functioning, well-being and ADHD symptoms improved in both groups, and the intervention group had a larger reduction in anxiety only. These interventions are group courses with homework and goal-setting calls, so they are a form of structured training and not a cue system.
- **[non-ADHD]** Shah, Friedman & Kruglanski (2002): six studies showed that activating a committed focal goal inhibits the accessibility of alternative goals, moderated by commitment, anxiety and depression, need for cognitive closure, and goal tenacity. Goal shielding had beneficial consequences for attainment. Kruglanski et al. (2002), "A theory of goal systems", is the broader framework. I retrieved no abstract for it, and I draw nothing from it. I found no ADHD test of goal shielding.
- **[non-ADHD]** Webb & Sheeran (2006): 47 experimental tests. A medium-to-large change in intention (d = 0.66) produced a small-to-medium change in behavior (d = 0.36). This quantifies the intention-behavior gap.

**Strength.** Reminder effect: strong in a lab, short-horizon, non-ADHD. ADHD link: correlational and trait-based. ADHD training trials: small, one RCT with no difference on the primary outcome. The project's term "goal neglect" matches Duncan's usage in name. Duncan's work concerns rule-following in structured tasks over minutes, and the product concerns intentions held for days. The evidence does not show that the same mechanism governs a stalled article.

**Implication (inference).** Reminders delivered at the point of action reduced neglect errors in the one clean experiment. Whether a reminder of an intention from last week behaves like a task-goal reminder every 12 trials is unknown.

## 4. Zeigarnik, open loops, and plan-making

**Claim.** Unfinished tasks are better remembered and keep intruding (Zeigarnik, 1927). Writing a specific plan for an unfinished goal ends the intrusion (Masicampo & Baumeister, 2011). Popular productivity writing turns this into "capture open loops to free your mind".

**Evidence.**
- **[general]** Ghibellini & Meier (2025) meta-analysis of the Zeigarnik and Ovsiankina effects: no memory advantage for unfinished tasks; a general tendency to resume interrupted tasks (Ovsiankina effect). The authors suggest the Zeigarnik effect depends on situational factors such as experimenter authority and task involvement. Measured: recall of interrupted versus completed tasks, resumption of interrupted tasks.
- **[workers]** Wendsche, Weigelt & Syrek (2026) meta-analysis: unfinished work tasks correlated with work-related thoughts in off-job time, ρ = .382 between persons (k = 17, N = 2,473) and ρ = .247 within persons (k = 14, N = 12,129). Strongest for affective rumination. Correlational.
- **[non-ADHD, participants not described in abstract]** Masicampo & Baumeister (2011, several studies): unfulfilled goals produced intrusive thoughts during an unrelated reading task, high accessibility of goal words, and poorer anagram performance. A specific plan eliminated these effects, and the reduction was mediated by how earnest the plan was; people who executed their plans were the ones with no more intrusions (Study 4). I found no direct replication in PubMed.
- **[ADHD]** No study of Zeigarnik, Ovsiankina or plan-induced relief in people with ADHD.

**Strength.** The memory claim failed in meta-analysis. The resumption tendency and the intrusion-by-unfinished-work link hold, with correlational designs. The plan-relief effect rests on one paper by two authors.

**Implication (inference).** Capturing a thought has not been tested as a way of closing a loop. The tested intervention was a specific plan with when/where/how, and a captured fragment is less than that.

- Zeigarnik, B. (1927). Das Behalten erledigter und unerledigter Handlungen. *Psychologische Forschung*, 9, 1–85. https://doi.org/10.1007/BF02409755 (cited via Wendsche et al.; original not retrieved)
- Ghibellini, R., & Meier, B. (2025). Interruption, recall and resumption: a meta-analysis of the Zeigarnik and Ovsiankina effects. *Humanities and Social Sciences Communications*, 12, 962. https://doi.org/10.1057/s41599-025-05000-w
- Wendsche, J., Weigelt, O., & Syrek, C. J. (2026). Unfinished work tasks and work-related thoughts during off-job time: meta-analysis of the Zeigarnik effect in a work-recovery context. *Anxiety, Stress, & Coping*, 39, 385–407. https://doi.org/10.1080/10615806.2026.2616302
- Masicampo, E. J., & Baumeister, R. F. (2011). Consider it done! Plan making can eliminate the cognitive effects of unfulfilled goals. *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, 101(4), 667–683. https://doi.org/10.1037/a0024192

## 5. Reward delay, motivation, dual-pathway models, novelty, interest, boredom

**Claim.** ADHD involves a motivational route to impairment alongside the executive one: preference for sooner rewards, aversion to delay, weaker response to infrequent reinforcement (Sonuga-Barke 2003; Sagvolden et al. 2005; Tripp & Wickens 2008; Zentall & Zentall 1983 for optimal stimulation).

**Official standards.** ICD-11 (6A05) defines inattention as significant difficulty sustaining attention on tasks that do not provide a high level of stimulation or frequent rewards. I read this text on a mirror (findacode.com), since the WHO browser did not render. DSM-5 criterion A for inattention includes failing to follow through on and finish duties; I confirmed the wording only on secondary sites and did not retrieve the APA text.

**Evidence.**
- **[ADHD, mixed ages]** Sonuga-Barke, Bitsakou & Thompson (2010): 71 probands (6–17), 71 siblings, 50 controls. Inhibitory control, delay aversion and temporal processing were independent components. Co-occurrence in ADHD was no greater than chance, and substantial groups showed only one problem. The authors propose a triple-pathway model and say it needs replication in larger samples. This shows that ADHD is heterogeneous: some people have the delay problem and some do not.
- **[ADHD, case-control meta-analysis]** Jackson & MacKillop (2016): 21 investigations, 25 comparisons, N = 3,913. Monetary delay discounting higher in ADHD, d = 0.43. No difference by age group (under vs over 18) or real vs hypothetical rewards; evidence of publication bias. Measured: choices between smaller sooner and larger later money.
- **[ADHD-child/adolescent]** Patros et al. (2016): 26 studies, 28 tasks, 4,320 children (2,360 ADHD). Choice impulsivity g = 0.47, with larger effects when diagnosis came from a single informant.
- **[ADHD-child/adolescent]** Marco et al. (2009): 360 probands, 349 siblings, 112 controls, 6–17. Smaller-sooner choice was higher in ADHD, and the effect was larger when the sooner choice shortened the trial. The authors read this as support for both an impulsive drive for immediate reward and delay aversion.
- **[ADHD-child]** Luman, Oosterlaan & Sergeant (2005) review: 22 studies, N = 1,181 children. Reinforcement improved performance and motivation in both ADHD and controls, somewhat more in ADHD. High intensity reinforcement was effective. Children with ADHD preferred immediate over delayed reward. The authors report a discrepancy between theoretical models and behavioral findings.
- **[ADHD-child]** Dovis et al. (2012): 30 ADHD vs 31 controls. Feedback alone was enough for controls, while children with ADHD needed more. The strongest incentives (10 euros, computer game version) normalized persistence over time and did not normalize working-memory performance; 1 euro did nothing.
- **[ADHD-adult]** Volkow et al. (2011): PET, 45 adults with ADHD and 41 controls, secondary analysis. The MPQ Achievement scale was lower in ADHD (11 ± 5 vs 14 ± 3) and correlated with D2/D3 receptor availability in accumbens (r = 0.39) and midbrain (r = 0.41) in the ADHD group only. Measured: a questionnaire scale as a surrogate for trait motivation, correlated with receptor availability. The common claim that dopamine deficit explains lack of motivation for dull tasks goes beyond a correlation of this size.
- **[ADHD, meta-analysis of personality]** Gomez & Corr (2014): up to 40 datasets. Inattention and hyperactivity/impulsivity both associated with low conscientiousness and high neuroticism. No effect sizes in the abstract.
- **[ADHD-child]** Donfrancesco et al. (2015): 146 children with ADHD vs 223 controls, parent-reported temperament. Novelty Seeking was higher in ADHD, also after removing impulsivity items. One study, one sample, parent report.
- **[ADHD, meta-analysis]** Muris, Otgaar & Donkers (2026): 18 mostly correlational studies, N = 22,365, boredom and ADHD r = 0.40 (95% CI 0.35–0.46).
- **[ADHD-adult, self-determination]** Morsink et al. (2022): a conceptual review stating that ADHD motivation research has focused on reinforcement and has given little attention to internal motives; it proposes self-determination theory as a frame.
- **[adult mediation]** Netzer Turgeman & Pollak (2026), preregistered, N = 640: sensitivity to delay, low perceived task value and flexible goal adjustment mediated the inattention–procrastination link. Oguchi et al. (2023), N = 58 university students: higher delay-discount rates went with more procrastination, and ADHD symptoms strengthened the link in a reward condition.
- **Term check.** "Interest-based nervous system" returns no peer-reviewed definition in PubMed or OpenAlex. A Zenodo record of the same phrase exists with no peer review (see Unconfirmed leads). The ICD-11 definition and the reinforcement and boredom findings are the nearest primary-literature support, and they concern stimulation and reward frequency and say nothing about "interest" as a construct.

**Strength.** Delay discounting: robust and replicated, medium effect, with publication bias flagged. Boredom: robust correlation, direction unknown. Novelty seeking: thin. Dopamine-motivation link: one secondary analysis. "Interest" as a driver: not directly tested.

**Implication (inference).** Pull toward immediate or stimulating work is documented at group level. Individual variation is large (Sonuga-Barke 2010 subgroups). A product that gives the maker something stimulating at the moment of action addresses the documented direction, and no study tests whether the old note itself is the stimulating item.

## 6. Hyperfocus and flow

**Claim.** People with ADHD can sustain very intense attention on activities that engage them. Flow (Csikszentmihalyi, 1990) is absorption in a challenge matched to skill, with clear goals and feedback.

**Evidence.**
- **[ADHD-adult, self-report]** Hupfeld, Abagis & Shah (2019): new Adult Hyperfocus Questionnaire. A pilot sample (n = 251) and replication sample (n = 372) of adults with and without ADHD. Higher ADHD symptoms went with more hyperfocus in school, hobbies and screen time. Preregistered hypothesis.
- **[ADHD-adult]** Ozel-Kizil et al. (2016): 53 stimulant-naive and 79 stimulant-treated adults with ADHD vs 65 healthy students. Hyperfocusing Scale higher in both ADHD groups (p < 0.001) and no difference between them. Controls were university students.
- **[ADHD-adult, contrary]** Groen et al. (2020): in 1,124 healthy adults, hyperfocus frequency correlated positively with ADHD traits. In 78 ADHD patients vs matched controls, occurrence, frequency, duration and pervasiveness did not differ, and hyperfocus was less likely in educational and social situations in patients. The authors conclude hyperfocus is not specific to ADHD patients.
- **[definition]** Ashinoff & Abu-Akel (2021): no consensus definition of hyperfocus; they propose an operational one.
- **[ADHD-adult gamers]** Pyszkowska et al. (2026): 401 gamers (174 with a formal ADHD diagnosis). Hyperfocus correlated with ADHD symptoms (rho = .386) and with flow (rho = .292). Gamers with ADHD scored higher on both hyperfocus and flow than gamers from the general population. In a second sample of 310 gamers with ADHD traits, hyperfocus predicted higher burnout and flow predicted higher self-efficacy.
- **[flow generally]** Peifer et al. (2022) scoping review of 252 flow studies (2000–2016) calls for more experimental and longitudinal work. Csikszentmihalyi (1990) is a book; I did not retrieve it.
- **[qualitative]** Sedgwick, Merwood & Asherson (2019): interviews with successful adults with ADHD; hyper-focus and divergent thinking were among the themes reported.
- I found no study of flow in adult ADHD makers or of hyperfocus predicting finished work.

**Strength.** Self-report, mostly cross-sectional, mixed on whether ADHD diagnosis predicts more hyperfocus. The popular description of hyperfocus as an ADHD strength is a claim the data do not support. Outcome data in gamers point to burnout for hyperfocus.

**Implication (inference).** The deep bursts the brief describes have some support as a self-reported experience. No source shows that bursts convert to finished output.

## 7. Plan-free theories: situated action, opportunistic planning, effectuation, emergent strategy

**Claim.** Competent action proceeds from the situation at hand and does not need a prior plan (Suchman, 1987). Planning itself proceeds opportunistically (Hayes-Roth & Hayes-Roth, 1979; Guindon, 1990). Strategy emerges from a pattern of actions and does not always follow a deliberate plan (Mintzberg & Waters, 1985). Expert entrepreneurs start from available means and let goals form (Sarasvathy, 2001).

**Evidence.**
- **[non-ADHD, cognitive modelling]** Hayes-Roth & Hayes-Roth (1979): a model in which planning specialists propose decisions whenever an opportunity appears, in no fixed order, illustrated with a think-aloud protocol and a computer simulation. Measured: protocol fit to the model.
- **[non-ADHD, designers]** Guindon (1990): verbal protocols of three professionals designing software. Top-down decomposition was problematic early in design; designers interleaved decisions across levels, triggered by newly discovered requirements and data-driven associations. N = 3.
- **[non-ADHD, theory]** Mintzberg & Waters (1985): deliberate and emergent strategy as two ends of a continuum, with typology from field research. Conceptual.
- **[non-ADHD, entrepreneurs]** Sarasvathy (2001) proposes effectuation as a theory. Dew, Read, Sarasvathy & Wiltbank (2009): 27 expert entrepreneurs and 37 MBA students thinking aloud; experts framed problems with effectual logic. Read, Song & Smit (2009) meta-analysis of articles in one journal, 9,897 new ventures: three effectuation principles positively connected to performance (abstract as reported in a search summary). Arend, Sarooghi & Burkemper (2015): effectuation has insufficient empirical testing and critical analysis, and they provide the first formal assessment of it as a theory.
- **[non-ADHD, critique]** Vera & Simon (1993) argue that situated-action claims that planning and representation are irrelevant are wrong, and that symbolic systems perform well in time-demanding tasks; Suchman (1993) replied. This is a live disagreement.
- **[ADHD]** I found no study applying any of these to ADHD.

**Strength.** Weak for the product's question. They describe skilled professionals in structured domains, and none was tested on people for whom planning is a documented difficulty. Effectuation is contested.

**Implication (inference).** The theories give a vocabulary for working from available means and incoming cues. They do not show that the approach produces finished work for the target person.

## 8. Does planning-based treatment work in adult ADHD

Included because the brief says planning fails. The trials below tested structured skills, many of which involve planners, lists and schedules.

**Evidence.**
- **[ADHD-adult]** Safren et al. (2010, JAMA): 86 medication-treated adults with persistent symptoms, 12 sessions of CBT vs relaxation with educational support. Assessor-blinded ratings improved more with CBT. Responders: 53% vs 23% (Clinical Global Impression, OR 3.80) and 67% vs 33% (ADHD Rating Scale, OR 4.29).
- **[ADHD-adult]** Solanto et al. (2010): 88 adults, 12-week group meta-cognitive therapy teaching time management, organization and planning vs supportive therapy. Response OR = 5.41 (95% CI 1.77–16.55) on blinded-evaluator ratings.
- **[ADHD-adult, meta-analysis]** Knouse, Teller & Brooks (2017): 32 studies, up to 896 participants. Versus control g = 0.65 for self-reported symptoms and 0.51 for self-reported functioning. Active control groups gave smaller effects. Longer treatments were not better. Effects were heterogeneous.
- **[ADHD-adult, Cochrane]** López et al. (2018): 14 RCTs, 700 participants. CBT vs waiting list: self-reported symptoms SMD −0.84 (moderate-quality evidence). CBT vs supportive therapy: self-reported symptoms SMD −0.16, not significant (low-quality).

**Strength.** Moderate, with high heterogeneity and effects that shrink against active comparators. These are multicomponent packages, and no study separates the effect of schedules and planners from the other ingredients. They do not test whether a person for whom scheduling failed in the past benefits from something else.

**Implication (inference).** The research contradicts a blanket "planning does not work for ADHD". The product's position can rest on the lack of evidence for a given individual, and the literature does not provide a basis for rejecting the planning treatments.

- Solanto, M. V., Marks, D. J., Wasserstein, J., et al. (2010). Efficacy of meta-cognitive therapy for adult ADHD. *American Journal of Psychiatry*, 167(8), 958–968. https://doi.org/10.1176/appi.ajp.2009.09081123

## 9. Why people with ADHD finish or ship little (question a, part 1)

**Claim.** Starting and finishing separate in ADHD. Self-regulation lapses, delay sensitivity and memory for intentions combine to produce unfinished work.

**Evidence.**
- **[standards]** DSM-5 criterion A lists failing to follow through and finish duties among nine inattention symptoms (secondary-site text; APA text not retrieved). Kessler et al. (2006): in a US household sample (N = 3,199, ages 18–44) estimated current adult ADHD prevalence 4.4%, associated with substantial role impairment. de Graaf et al. (2008): in 10 national surveys (7,075 workers), ADHD was associated with 22.1 annual days of excess lost role performance.
- **[entrepreneurship, meta-analysis]** Tran, Wiklund, Antshel et al. (2025, online): 47 studies, 298 effect sizes. Hyperactivity/impulsivity positively associated with entrepreneurial attitudes and behaviors and not significantly with post-launch outcomes; inattention negatively associated with post-launch outcomes; total symptoms and diagnosis positively related to entrepreneurial behaviors and negatively to outcomes. Wiklund et al. (2017): inattention was negatively and hyperactivity positively associated with entrepreneurship; sensation seeking and lack of premeditation positively. This is the closest data to "starts a lot, ships little", and the outcomes are business outcomes, not creative output.
- **[procrastination]** Niermann & Scheres (2014): 54 students, only inattention (corrected for the other domain) correlated with general procrastination. Altgassen et al. (2019): 29 adults with ADHD vs 24 controls; everyday prospective memory partially mediated the link between ADHD symptoms and procrastination. Netzer Turgeman & Pollak (2026): N = 640; delay sensitivity, low perceived task value and flexible goal adjustment mediated. Steel (2007) meta-analysis (691 correlations, non-ADHD): task aversiveness, task delay, self-efficacy, impulsiveness and conscientiousness facets were strong predictors; neuroticism, rebelliousness and sensation seeking were weak.
- **[creative output]** Hoogman et al. (2020): review of 31 behavioral studies. Most found increased divergent thinking with high subclinical ADHD scores and not with the clinical disorder; creative achievement rates were high in clinical and subclinical groups; no evidence of better convergent thinking; no overall negative effect of stimulants on creativity. Boot, Nevicka & Baas (2020): adults with ADHD reported more real-world creative achievements, no group difference in intrinsic motivation during idea generation, and more original ideas when competing for a bonus; the authors propose goal-directed motivation as the driver. Self-report of achievement, small designs.
- **[publishing/finishing creative work]** I found no study that measures publishing, public sharing, or completion of self-initiated creative projects in ADHD.

**Strength.** Association is consistent across studies and samples (mostly students and online samples). Mechanism evidence is mediation in cross-sectional data. The entrepreneurship meta-analysis is large and heterogeneous in constructs.

**Implication (inference).** Starting is not the bottleneck in the entrepreneurship data. The documented gap is after launch. Procrastination data tie the gap to memory for intentions and to delay sensitivity, which are both addressed by cues at the moment of action if the cue works as intended.

## 10. Fear of negative evaluation, perfectionism, social-evaluative threat (question a, part 2)

**Claim.** Publishing exposes work to judgment, and people with ADHD, who have received more criticism, avoid it or fail to finish to avoid it.

**Evidence.**
- **[general population, lab]** Dickerson & Kemeny (2004): meta-analysis of 208 laboratory studies of acute stressors. Motivated-performance tasks raised cortisol if they were uncontrollable or involved social-evaluative threat (performance can be negatively judged by others); tasks with both produced the largest cortisol and ACTH changes and longest recovery. Measured: cortisol in lab tasks (such as public speaking). The common claim that being judged is a threat response is supported at the hormone level in lab tasks. It does not measure avoidance of sharing work.
- **[ADHD, criticism]** Beaton, Sirois & Milne (2022): 162 adults with ADHD or high ADHD traits wrote about criticism. Inattentive behaviors were most criticized; criticism was reported to harm self-worth and wellbeing; some avoided criticism. Beaton, Sirois & Milne (2020): 1,203 adults (46% self-reported diagnosis); ADHD diagnosis associated with lower self-compassion and higher perceived criticism, and criticism partly explained the self-compassion difference. Cross-sectional, recruited online.
- **[ADHD, social anxiety]** Jakobsson Støre et al. (2024) systematic review of 41 articles: prevalence of social anxiety disorder in those with ADHD ranged from 0.04% to 49.5%, studies were mostly of weak quality, and co-occurrence went with greater impairment. Fredrick et al. (2020), N = 4,756 university students: fear of negative evaluation and social anxiety were linked more strongly at higher sluggish cognitive tempo, and ADHD inattention symptoms did not moderate the link. The prevalence range is too wide to support a number.
- **[ADHD, self-handicapping]** Waschbusch et al. (2007): 61 children (22 ADHD on placebo, 21 ADHD on stimulant, 18 controls). Children with ADHD showed more self-handicapping before academic tasks and more positive self-evaluations of performance, regardless of medication. Jaconis et al. (2016): 699 college students; claimed and behavioral self-handicapping related to ADHD symptoms and impairment. Self-handicapping is a face-saving pre-emptive excuse. It is adjacent to fear of evaluation and not the same measure.
- **[ADHD, perfectionism]** I found no adult study of perfectionism and publishing in ADHD. Katzenmajer-Pump et al. (2021): 88 adolescents with ADHD vs 96 controls; the ADHD group scored lower on adaptive "Organization" perfectionism and not on other traits, and lower perfectionism, particularly personal standards, was associated with suicidal behavior. Christian et al. (2021), N = 1,353 undergraduates: impulsivity and perfectionism co-occurred in a large subgroup. The Steel (2007) abstract does not list perfectionism among the strong predictors of procrastination (task aversiveness, delay, self-efficacy, impulsiveness, conscientiousness facets) and reports neuroticism as weak.
- **[ADHD, self-perception]** Owens et al. (2007) review of the positive illusory bias in children with ADHD (inflated self-perceptions). Canu & Carlson (2007) propose that it could buffer rejection-sensitivity development (see section 11).

**Strength.** Social-evaluative threat: strong in non-ADHD lab research. ADHD-specific evaluation fear: indirect and low quality. Perfectionism: no support for a general claim, and one adolescent study points toward lower perfectionism.

**Implication (inference).** The research gives a plausible route for evaluation fear and does not show that it is the reason makers with ADHD do not publish.

## 11. Rejection sensitivity: what the primary literature supports

**Status.** "Rejection sensitive dysphoria" (RSD) is not a diagnosis in DSM-5-TR or ICD-11. In the ICD-11 description I read, emotional dysregulation is not mentioned. Rejection sensitivity (RS) is a separate, validated social-cognition construct: Downey & Feldman (1996) define people sensitive to rejection as those who "anxiously expect, readily perceive, and overreact to it", measured with a scale for anxious expectations of rejection.

**Evidence.**
- **[general]** Gao et al. (2017) meta-analysis: 75 studies; RS correlated with depression (r = 0.33), anxiety (0.41), loneliness (0.39), borderline personality disorder (0.41) and body dysmorphic disorder (0.43); similar in clinical and community samples.
- **[ADHD-young adult men]** Canu & Carlson (2007): 31 combined-type, 22 inattentive-type and 25 non-diagnosed male undergraduates. The hypothesis that ADHD would have elevated RS was not supported. Low RS predicted divergent outcomes across groups. The authors suggest the positive illusory bias could buffer RS.
- **[ADHD-adolescents]** Bondü & Esser (2015): 1,235 German 10–19-year-olds. Those with ADHD symptoms reported higher anxious and angry rejection sensitivity and higher victim justice sensitivity. RS partially mediated the link between ADHD symptoms and comorbid problems.
- **[ADHD, college, 2026]** de Arellano et al. (2026), N = 4,150 US students: ADHD symptoms and RS correlated positively, and RS weakened the link between ADHD symptoms and cannabis use. The authors state there are mixed findings on RS and ADHD symptoms.
- **[ADHD, qualitative]** Rowney-Smith et al. (2026): 5 undergraduates in focus groups; themes of withdrawal, masking and bodily sensations. Beaton et al. (2022), above.
- **[emotion regulation reviews]** Shaw et al. (2014): emotion dysregulation is prevalent in ADHD across the lifespan and contributes to impairment; consensus on conceptualization is missing. Faraone et al. (2019): emotional symptoms are common and persistent in youth and adults with ADHD; there is no consensus on how to define constructs specifically enough to use as diagnostic criteria.
- **[critical]** van Asselt (2026), a critical reflection in a Dutch psychiatry journal: RSD is a social-media-popularized term, introduced by William Dodson from clinical experience; the author lists limited empirical evidence among four risks and recommends the established rejection-sensitivity spectrum instead.

**What is established.** (1) Emotion dysregulation is common in ADHD. (2) RS is a real, measurable construct with moderate links to anxiety and depression in general samples. (3) ADHD and RS correlate in some samples (adolescents, college students) and an adult male sample did not show elevated RS. (4) Adults with ADHD report more criticism and describe harm from it.
**What is not established.** RSD as a distinct condition; RS as an ADHD-specific symptom; any effect of RS on publishing.

## 12. Spontaneous thought and cue-driven retrieval

**Evidence.**
- **[ADHD, non-clinical samples and one clinical sample]** Seli et al. (2015): two non-clinical samples (N = 1,354 each) and a clinical ADHD sample. Spontaneous mind wandering (an executive-control failure) and not deliberate mind wandering is central to ADHD symptomatology.
- **[review]** Kvavilashvili & Rummel (2020) integrate evidence on mind wandering, future thinking and prospective memory, including involuntary future thinking.
- Note 09 covers focal-cue spontaneous retrieval (McDaniel & Einstein) and the finding that related material resurfacing does not by itself change behavior.

**Strength.** Self-report of mind wandering. No work tests resurfacing old ideas during scrolling.

- Seli, P., Smallwood, J., Cheyne, J. A., & Smilek, D. (2015). On the relation of mind wandering and ADHD symptomatology. *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review*, 22, 629–636. https://doi.org/10.3758/s13423-014-0793-0
- Kvavilashvili, L., & Rummel, J. (2020). On the nature of everyday prospection: A review and theoretical integration of research on mind-wandering, future thinking, and prospective memory. *Review of General Psychology*, 24, 210–237. https://doi.org/10.1177/1089268020918843

## Unconfirmed leads

Sources are non-primary or I could not trace a primary source.

- **"Interest-based nervous system."** A coaching and social-media term, associated with William Dodson (attribution unverified here). A Zenodo record titled as a question about this model exists with no peer review, which I did not treat as a source. The nearest primary support is in section 5 (ICD-11 wording; reinforcement and boredom findings).
- **"Rejection sensitive dysphoria."** Dodson-coined per van Asselt (2026), which is itself a critical commentary. The primary literature supports "rejection sensitivity" (section 11).
- **Body doubling.** A PubMed search for the phrase returned no relevant studies. Claims about effectiveness come from coaching and social media.
- **"Time blindness."** Popular label. Primary support exists for timing deficits (Noreika et al., 2013) and for time-based PM deficits in children, and not for the label's broader claims. Barkley's formulation is in his books, which I did not retrieve.
- **Barkley "point of performance."** Barkley's claim that ADHD is a disorder of performance and that supports belong at the point of performance appears in his books (for example *ADHD and the Nature of Self-Control*, Guilford, 1997). I did not retrieve them. His 1997 *Psychological Bulletin* paper (https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.121.1.65) is the journal version of the theory, and I did not retrieve its abstract.
- **David Allen, *Getting Things Done* (2001) on open loops.** The original book states the claim and I did not retrieve it. The closest empirical test is Masicampo & Baumeister (2011).
- **Dopamine menus, task paralysis, ADHD tax.** Coaching terms with no primary source found in this search.

## What the literature does not answer

These questions have no direct evidence in the sources above. The human will reason about them.

1. Whether any intervention works without a schedule for adults with ADHD who are makers. No trial tests a schedule-free design in adult ADHD, in makers, or over weeks.
2. Whether cues chosen by software (an old note beside the cursor, a prompt in a feed) change the next act in adults with ADHD. Every cue study used cues specified by the experimenter or by the participant ahead of time.
3. Whether event-based cues beat time-based reminders as interventions in ADHD. Evidence compares performance on memory tasks, in children mostly, and adult results disagree.
4. Whether resurfacing old ideas while someone is scrolling or consuming adds output or takes attention from the current task. No study.
5. Whether capturing a thought, without a specific when/where/how plan, relieves the cognitive load of an unfinished goal in ADHD or in anyone. The only supportive paper tested specific plans.
6. Whether a note from days or weeks ago works as a goal reminder. The reminder experiments used task goals repeated every few trials.
7. Whether hyperfocus or flow predicts finished publishable work in ADHD. Evidence is self-report of the experience, with burnout as an outcome in gamers.
8. Whether fear of negative evaluation, perfectionism or rejection sensitivity stops people with ADHD from publishing. No study of publishing, and the social anxiety prevalence range is too wide to use.
9. Whether lowering the cost of starting (one click) raises completion. Entrepreneurship data show starting and outcomes separate in ADHD, and no study varies the cost of starting.
10. Whether results for diagnosed samples apply to the "scattered ambitious" person who may not have a diagnosis. Samples are diagnosed, trait-scored, or self-diagnosed, and one 2026 study found systematic differences between formally diagnosed and self-diagnosed groups.
11. How many prompts a person tolerates before they are ignored. No data on habituation to cues in ADHD.
12. Whether publishing itself, repeated, lowers evaluation fear (exposure), or raises it. No study in ADHD.
13. Whether the product helps people who already respond to planning treatments. Section 8 shows that planning training helps on average, and whether a cue-based product adds to or substitutes for it is unknown.
14. Whether old ideas are the stimulating item in the sense of sections 5 and 6 (novelty, interest, reward). Novelty and reinforcement were manipulated in child lab tasks with money or games, and no study manipulated resurfaced personal ideas.
