# Load-bearing failure

## Gist

Sources do not crown one of the five candidate failures as the single load-bearing cause. The closest upstream failure is **losing active control by formed intentions and goals** under concurrent load — what Duncan calls goal neglect and the prospective-memory literature treats as intention maintenance and monitoring, not mere recall. Holding many projects and context switching are treated as amplifiers and costs; organizing thoughts is largely compensatory; inability to stay on one thing is treated as downstream.

---

## How the constructs map to the candidate failures

| Candidate failure | Primary constructs in the literature |
|---|---|
| Losing hold of intentions already formed | Prospective memory, goal neglect, executive monitoring |
| The pain of organizing thoughts | Task-model construction, cognitive/intention offloading |
| Context switching itself | Task switching, attention residue, task-set reconfiguration |
| Holding many projects at once | Task-model complexity, goal neglect load, multitasking |
| The inability to stay on one thing | Sustained attention / persistence, behavioral inhibition |

## Losing hold of intentions (prospective memory and goal neglect)

**What the sources claim.** Prospective memory is the execution of delayed intentions while doing other work (Einstein & McDaniel, 1990). Everyday memory failures are disproportionately prospective: diary studies cited in Gilbert (2022) put delayed-intention failures at roughly 50–70% of reported memory problems. Holding an intention while performing ongoing activity imposes measurable capacity cost: participants with embedded prospective-memory intentions slow on non-target trials, and greater slowing predicts better prospective-memory performance (Smith, 2003).

**Cause or consequence.** In the multiprocess framework, top-down processes that maintain activation of the intention and/or monitor for target cues are upstream requirements for successful remembering; spontaneous cue-driven retrieval can substitute under some conditions but still depends on prior encoding and cue conditions (McDaniel & Einstein, 2000; McDaniel, Umanath, Einstein, & Waldum, 2015). Smith (2003) treats preparatory monitoring as a cause of successful intention fulfillment, not a side effect.

**Goal neglect refines “losing hold.”** Duncan, Emslie, Williams, Johnson, & Freer (1996) introduced goal neglect: a person can understand and explicitly recall a task requirement yet fail to apply it in behavior — subjectively, the requirement “slips the subject’s mind.” Duncan et al. (2008) argue that as instructions are assembled into a limited-capacity “task model,” components compete for representation and vulnerable rules may be lost from behavioral control even when still available for explicit recall. Duncan (2012) links this rule-working-memory failure to fluid intelligence more strongly than several traditional working-memory spans.

**ADHD complication.** Fuermaier et al. (2013) found large impairments in *task planning* for adults with ADHD on a complex prospective-memory paradigm, but plan recall after a 40-minute delay was intact (~86% in both groups). Self-initiation and plan fidelity were not significantly impaired after correction for multiple comparisons. The authors conclude complex prospective-memory failure in this sample “mainly emerged from deficient planning abilities,” not from losing encoded intentions from memory. A 2025 virtual-reality study of time-based prospective memory in children with ADHD found group differences were fully mediated by less *strategic* time monitoring, not by the retrospective component of remembering what to do (Kangas et al., 2025; abstract and full text read via PMC).

**Implication for the ticket.** “Losing hold of intentions” is the best plain-language match to the upstream literature, but sources split *recall* from *control*: the load-bearing problem is often that intentions stop governing behavior (goal neglect, failed monitoring/planning), not that they are forgotten.

## Holding many projects at once (task-model complexity)

**What the sources claim.** Duncan et al. (2008) show goal neglect rises with the total complexity of instructions — the number of rules in the whole task model — even when participants know some rules will not be needed in the current block. Duncan (2012) reports neglect is driven by “the total complexity of the whole set of rules described in the initial instructions,” not by real-time processing demands on a single trial.

**Cause or consequence.** Multiple concurrent requirements are treated as a **load condition that amplifies neglect**, not as the fundamental deficit. Fuermaier et al. (2013) use a ten-subtest planning paradigm precisely because daily life “often multiple tasks need to be integrated in one plan”; impairment appeared in forming the plan, not in retaining it.

**Implication.** “Many open projects” matches the complexity variable in goal-neglect and complex prospective-memory work, but sources position it as what overwhelms executive control of intentions rather than as the deepest failure in itself.

- Duncan et al. (2012), as above
- Fuermaier et al. (2013), as above

## Context switching itself (task switching and attention residue)

**What the sources claim.** Monsell (2003) reviews task switching: responses are slower and more error-prone immediately after a switch (“switch cost”), reduced but not eliminated by preparation. Switch costs reflect task-set reconfiguration plus carry-over of activation and inhibition from prior task-sets.

Leroy (2009) introduces *attention residue*: cognitions about a prior task persist after switching, creating cognitive load that hurts performance on the next task. Unfinished prior tasks make disengagement harder; merely finishing is not always enough without time pressure at closure.

**Cause or consequence.** Task switching is treated as an **operational demand with measurable costs** (Monsell, 2003) and as a **situation that exposes or worsens intention-control failures** (Duncan et al., 1996, used prospective-memory letter-monitoring with switching rules; neglect increased when a second prospective task was added). Attention residue is a **consequence of transitions**, especially from unfinished work (Leroy, 2009). Fuermaier et al. (2013) found impaired “task switching” performance in ADHD, but correlated it with planning and plan fidelity — switching here indexes overall prospective-memory execution, not switching as root cause.

**Implication.** Context switching is load-bearing for *performance in the moment*, but the literature generally treats it as downstream of how well intentions and task models are maintained across transitions.

- Monsell, S. (2003). Task switching. *Trends in Cognitive Sciences*, 7(3), 134–140. https://doi.org/10.1016/S1364-6613(03)00028-7 (full text read via course PDF mirror)
- Leroy, S. (2009). Why is it so hard to do my work? The challenge of attention residue when switching between work tasks. *Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes*, 109(2), 168–181. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.obhdp.2009.04.002 (full text read)
- Duncan et al. (1996), as above
- Fuermaier et al. (2013), as above

## The pain of organizing thoughts (externalizing and task-model construction)

**What the sources claim.** Gilbert (2022) reviews intention offloading: calendars, notes, and reminders are effective extensions of prospective memory, chosen more when memory load and interruption risk are high. Offloading is guided by metacognitive beliefs about forgetting, not only objective memory ability. Risko and Gilbert (2016), cited in that review, define cognitive offloading as using physical action to reduce a task’s cognitive demand.

Duncan et al. (2012) describe constructing a “mental control program” from instructions — separating rules, assembling task parts — as the demanding step that links to fluid intelligence.

**Cause or consequence.** Organizing and externalizing are treated as **compensatory strategies** triggered when internal maintenance is costly or unreliable (Gilbert, 2022), and as the **effortful upstream work of building a task model** before behavior can be reliably governed (Duncan et al., 2012). The “pain” is not named as a primary construct; it aligns with executive and metacognitive labor when many intentions must be structured.

**Implication.** Sources treat thought-organization pain as effort spent coping with limited intention-control capacity, not as the deepest failure.

- Gilbert, S. J., Boldt, A., Sachdeva, C., Scarampi, C., & Tsai, P.-C. (2022). Outsourcing memory to external tools: A review of ‘intention offloading’. *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review*, 29, 2117–2130. https://doi.org/10.3758/s13423-022-02139-4 (full text read)
- Duncan et al. (2012), as above

## Inability to stay on one thing (persistence and sustained attention)

**What the sources claim.** Barkley (1997) proposes behavioral inhibition as the central ADHD deficit; working memory, internalized speech, affect/motivation regulation, and reconstitution depend on inhibition and enable behavior to be governed by internally represented goals over time. Apparent sustained-attention deficits are reinterpreted as reduced control of behavior by those executive representations.

Miyake et al. (2000) factor executive function into correlated but separable updating, inhibition, and shifting; Friedman & Miyake (2012, review summarized on NCBI Bookshelf) describe unity and diversity — a common EF factor plus separable components.

**Cause or consequence.** In Barkley’s hierarchy, persistence failures are **downstream of inhibition and executive-function breakdown**. In prospective-memory work, staying on an ongoing task while maintaining an intention creates measurable dual-task cost (Smith, 2003) — persistence and intention maintenance **compete for capacity**. Leroy (2009) treats poor focus after a switch as residue from prior tasks, not as a standalone primary deficit.

**Implication.** “Cannot stay on one thing” is widely modeled as a symptom or performance consequence of intention-control and switching dynamics, not the root failure named across frameworks.

- Barkley, R. A. (1997). Behavioral inhibition, sustained attention, and executive functions: Constructing a unifying theory of ADHD. *Psychological Bulletin*, 121(1), 65–94. https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.121.1.65 (full text read)
- Miyake, A., Friedman, N. P., Emerson, M. J., Witzki, A. H., Howerter, A., & Wager, T. D. (2000). The unity and diversity of executive functions and their contributions to complex “frontal lobe” tasks: A latent variable analysis. *Cognitive Psychology*, 41(1), 49–100. https://doi.org/10.1006/cogp.1999.0734 (abstract and secondary summaries; full text not retrieved)
- Friedman, N. P., & Miyake, A. (2017); Friedman, N. P., & Robbins, T. W. (2022). Unity and diversity of executive functions. In *The Frontal Cortex* (NCBI Bookshelf NBK609780) — secondary synthesis read
- Smith (2003); Leroy (2009), as above

## Cause vs. consequence (synthesis)

| Candidate failure | Typical role in sources |
|---|---|
| Losing hold of intentions | **Closest upstream failure**, especially as loss of *behavioral control* by intentions (goal neglect) and failed monitoring/planning; pure recall loss is often *not* the story in ADHD complex-PM data |
| Holding many projects at once | **Amplifier** — raises task-model complexity and competition among goals |
| Context switching | **Cost and exposure mechanism** — switch costs, residue, and neglected switching rules when intentions are weak |
| Pain of organizing thoughts | **Compensatory labor** — offloading and task-model construction under load |
| Inability to stay on one thing | **Downstream symptom** — persistence breakdown, dual-task interference, attention residue |

---

## Do sources crown a single winner?

**No.** Frameworks disagree on the deepest mechanism:

- **Goal-neglect / task-model account (Duncan):** failure to keep all specified requirements actively controlling behavior when the task model is complex.
- **Multiprocess prospective memory (McDaniel & Einstein):** both strategic monitoring and spontaneous retrieval can support intentions; which dominates depends on cue focality and task demands.
- **ADHD complex-PM data (Fuermaier et al., 2013):** planning deficit with intact plan retention — challenges a simple “intentions fall out of memory” story.
- **Behavioral inhibition hierarchy (Barkley, 1997):** inhibition impairment as central, with working memory and persistence as dependent; later work treats working memory as a correlated core deficit rather than purely downstream (e.g., Panah et al., 2024, cited in a Frontiers review — not primary text read here).

What converges: the scattered-ambitious pattern — many concurrent intentions, heavy switching, weak behavioral grip on what was already decided — is best described in the literature as **executive failure to keep formed intentions and task requirements actively governing behavior under concurrent load**, with multiplicity and switching as conditions that expose and worsen that failure. No primary source reviewed here elevates “organizing thoughts” or “context switching alone” above that.
