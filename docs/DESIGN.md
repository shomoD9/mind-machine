## Native capture/refind architecture

A generated, committed Xcode project links those libraries into three shipping targets and a simulator-only keyboard test host; separate XCUITest runners drive that host and the Mac app. The Mac AppKit adapter registers Control–Option–Space through Carbon and hosts the shared SwiftUI workspace in a floating resizable panel; Escape hides the panel without replacing its model. The companion provides scrolling setup content and a bottom safe-area composer. This representative host keeps its composer directly above the entire keyboard workspace as the extension grows; setup content scrolls away first. The keyboard controller owns a private UIKit editor and a KeyboardKit keyplane, routes deliberate host input through `textDocumentProxy`, and exposes original inspection with explicit Insert. The keys and their routing are described in "Native-feel keyboard and greyscale UI" below.

Phone targets resolve the same entitled App Group URL before constructing the model. The keyboard requires Full Access and refuses an isolated fallback vault if sharing is unavailable. App Groups share the SQLite store on one device; private CloudKit replication exchanges app-encrypted revisions across paired devices. Real signed-device exchange remains an acceptance gate. Ordinary host typing is never appended as a capture. Save confirmation follows a durable SQLite transaction; Recall returns complete originals with a separate highlighted preview. The pure saved-vault test reopens storage using another caller before querying it. VaultFile owns the indexed FTS5/BM25 prefix/typo path used by keyboard and panel. The legacy pure Vault ranking helper remains for compatibility tests; neural semantic retrieval is outside the typing path and remains a gap. ## What is already decided

- The spine is goal neglect. Intentions stop governing action under the load of many open projects. Many projects and context switching amplify it. Organizing thoughts is compensation. Inability to stay on one thing is downstream.
- The pitch is one product with two moments. Capture takes parallel bursts and the person does not classify. Retrieval has to change the next act. That change happens at the moment of action, when a cue retrieves the intended act. Capture, a related note beside the cursor, and a later review can all leave notes that are never acted on.
- The pitch will include a day in the life and a rough interface.
- Capture requires adjacent writing spaces so a new thought can be opened while another remains unfinished. Related notes during capture are optional and easy to ignore.
- The capture overlay blurs and darkens the background and uses a shortcut to open and dismiss it. Wikilinks are a first-class interaction: select a word or phrase and use a simple hotkey to create a linked dialog. Both belong in the MVP at the level of behavior agreed so far. A wikilink creates an independent note, not a fragment of its parent. A multi-thought block gets an optional split hint. A card shows a backend chunk of the block, and the hint shows the maker the proposed boundary so they can fix it. Accepting or adjusting the hint is Explode, and it is in the MVP. The standalone Explode hotkey is beyond the MVP. Capture in the MVP is typing only; the maker can dictate with their own tool, and native speech capture is later.
- Explode splits a large text block into atomic notes by meaning. In the MVP it happens by accepting the split hint. The standalone hotkey is part of the product vision and outside the MVP.
- Capture preserves separate thoughts and the maker's wording, allowing minor corrections for legibility. It retains a source or draft reference when available; inferred connections remain revisable. These choices are recorded in [What capture must preserve](../.scratch/scattered-ambitious-pitch/issues/10-what-capture-must-preserve.md), whose interaction decisions remain open.
- The first buyer is the maker. They stay because the dump and the point of decision are one product. They tell another maker, and they tell from pride. Passive productivity names the offer: the upkeep is calm, and the start is one click. The piece is made after that.
- The form is a point of decision. A dumped note appears inside an app the maker already has open. One click takes it to a place they chose. There is no home they must remember to open. A rare label correction does not gate that click. The price band is the utility band, about $3 to $12 a month, with a free tier where the maker brings their own API keys. Notes live on the maker's device by default. The paid tier adds cross-device sync and managed AI, so a paying maker needs no keys. The exact price inside the band is open. The list of apps and points of decision is still ahead.
- The effort refuses clinical treatment and a general productivity suite for everyone.
- Decided 2026-10-02: the spine is spontaneity. The maker works from the thought that arrives, with no schedules or timetables. Goal neglect stays as the problem statement for why planned work fails. The founder is the first user and the claim is that solving for self solves for many neurodivergent people. Research on reaching goals without scheduled action is [15-goals-without-planning](../.scratch/scattered-ambitious-pitch/research/15-goals-without-planning.md), with first-principles reasoning to follow.

- Decided 2026-10-02: notes tagged private by a small local model are captured and recalled but never turned into prompts and never leave the device. The daily canvas (scrollable earlier days, the day's notes in the background, a central agent-written note) is a browsing view and is never required. The first surfaces are prompts in the X timeline and Substack as the publishing target; GitHub and code come after.

It is quiet while the maker types, appears when they pause or on a hotkey, and shows at most one card. Clicking it inserts the note's text at the cursor, with a secondary control that opens it as a dialog.

Captured thoughts are sorted by background agents into two wikis, a private wiki of the self and a wiki of ideas, where the ideas wiki never links to the self wiki. Pages are told as stories. Capture dialogs are an aid and not the stored structure. A note is not marked posted by a click: the product asks the maker later and reads public surfaces on a cadence to reconcile. The daily canvas's central note is a mirror of the day. The MVP is Mac only.

### Private replication and signing — 23 October 2026

`NoteRevision` is immutable and records parent revision IDs. `VaultFile` queues changes in the same SQLite transaction as the source/index. Merge keeps concurrent head revisions as deterministic original copies, and branch-local tombstones remove only their named heads, retaining unseen concurrent edits. Deferred reminder/chat metadata stays local. `RevisionCipher` authenticates each revision using AES-256-GCM with a versioned context. A shared 256-bit key is installed explicitly through `PrivateSyncSetupView`; synchronizable Keychain with a shared access group supplies the app/keyboard and signed Mac. Create once and pair the same recovery key on the other device; never auto-create independent keys during startup. Losing all copies loses cloud decryption; retain a private recovery copy.

`PrivateCloudSync` uses CKSyncEngine (iOS 17/macOS 14 baseline) and the private database/custom zone. Only the app ciphertext is put in `CKRecord.encryptedValues["sealedRevision"]`, retaining service-unreadable content independently of Advanced Data Protection. User record identity binds to the resident vault before exchange; sign-out/account switch pauses and never erases/reuploads into another account. Per-client engine cursors share a durable revision outbox, with authenticated duplicate-upload acknowledgement. Cloud/decryption failures retain pending writes and local originals. Corrupt incoming data stops cursor advancement. Active clients request foreground sync on startup, commits and the periodic timer; keyboard background lifetime is OS-controlled and no background deadline is promised.

### Keyboard feel parity — 24 October 2026

The founder asked for the iPhone keys to look and feel like Apple's keyboard. `docs/verification/keyboard-feel-2026-10-24.md` audits the keyboard against Apple's iOS 26 keyboard (15 gaps, G1–G15) and records what closed each one. KeyboardKit 9.9.1 stays the base and stays unmodified; the changes go through its public hooks and two subclasses.

**Where the behaviour lives.** `KeyplaneView` adjusts the layout, styles and callouts KeyboardKit draws. `KeyplaneBehavior.swift` holds `KeyplaneBehavior` (a `StandardKeyboardBehavior` subclass that decides how much one delete removes) and `KeyplaneActionHandler` (a `StandardActionHandler` subclass that sees every key gesture first). `KeyplaneState` is shared by the controller, the view and the handler: it carries the return key's disabled flag, the frame of every visible key cell and the time the current delete hold began. `KeyAlternates.swift` is the long-press table. The controller installs the behaviour and handler in `configureKeyboardKit`.

**Geometry.** CI captures this keyboard and Apple's on the same simulators (`KeyboardTestHost --apple-keyboard-reference`), and pixel measurements set the iOS 26 presets: Apple's faces sit 7.33 pt apart and 7.33 pt from the screen edge on every phone; portrait rows are 54 pt (phones narrower than 428 pt) or 56 pt (Pro Max) with 5.5 pt above and below each face; landscape rows are 35.33 pt with 4 pt. `AppleGeometry.install()` writes these into KeyboardKit's device-configuration presets once, before any layout, because KeyboardKit reads the same presets for both key layout and callout placement. KeyboardKit draws a face 1 pt inside its configured horizontal inset, so the inset is 2.67 pt, and `KeyplaneView` pads the keyplane by 3.67 pt on each side. Before iOS 26 KeyboardKit's own presets apply unchanged. After the change the installed extension's faces land within one pixel of Apple's on the iPhone 17 Pro and Pro Max simulators.

**Long-press alternates.** Only the eleven letters Apple gives alternates (a c e i l n o s u y z) show a callout, with the iOS 17+ English (US) sets. KeyboardKit's open-source table also offered d g h k r t w and repeated `u`. Each list starts with the plain letter, as KeyboardKit's did, so lifting without sliding types it; whether Apple's popup includes the plain letter could not be settled from sources and is a device check. Digits and punctuation keep KeyboardKit's table.

**Delete.** Touch-down deletes one character and the hold repeats every 0.1 s after 0.5 s, as before. After 2.5 s of holding, each repeat removes a whole word, at most five words a second (`DeleteRepeat`). KeyboardKit switched at 3.5 s and kept the 0.1 s pace, which jumps from 10 to roughly 50 characters a second. Apple publishes no timings; these values are tuned by feel and need a phone to confirm.

**Shift and 123.** Shift shows capitals on touch-down. Letters tapped while shift is held stay capital and shift returns to lowercase on release; a plain tap still goes through KeyboardKit, so double-tap caps lock is unchanged. Touching shift or 123, sliding onto a key or the space bar and lifting types it, then returns to the earlier case or layer. The handler finds the key under the finger from the cell frames `KeyplaneView` publishes as a SwiftUI preference, which always describe the layer on screen. The key host accepts multiple touches so a held shift and roll-over typing both reach the keys.

**Return key.** An action return (Go, Search, Send, Done…) takes the prominent grey used by Save note instead of Apple's blue, keeping the keyboard greyscale. When the host field sets `enablesReturnKeyAutomatically` and holds no text, return shows a faint label and ignores taps, as Apple's does in Messages or a search field. `RoutedTextProxy` now forwards the destination's return-key traits; the private note keeps UIKit's defaults (return, never disabled).

**Landscape.** In a compact vertical size class the note is 32, 44 or 88 pt tall (Min, Default, Expanded), the destination and formatting row moves beside the mode switcher, and the extension requests only what is visible: four key rows (35.33 pt each on iOS 26), 104 pt of header, actions, margins and spacing, plus the note and any results. In landscape iOS places the globe and dictation keys beside a third-party keyboard, so the keyboard's width there is the system's choice and needs checking on a phone. Note and results heights sit below required priority, so a host that grants less height squeezes results, then the note, never the keys.

**Haptics and sound.** Haptic generators are prepared again after each tap so the next one is not delayed. Sound remains one `playInputClick` for every key (the only public API that follows Keyboard Feedback → Sound), and haptics follow System Haptics because third-party keyboards cannot read Keyboard Feedback → Haptic. Both are platform limits.

**Tests.** `KeyboardFeelTests` (second file of `MindMachineKeyboardUITests`) drives the same host through real gestures: double-tap caps lock, an accented e from a long-press slide, no accent on t, slides from shift and 123, the grey Search key (pixel-sampled), the inert return key, delete reaching whole words on a word boundary, and the landscape panel. It also saves screenshots of this keyboard and of Apple's keyboard on the same simulator in both orientations: `KeyboardTestHost --apple-keyboard-reference` focuses a plain field so Apple's keyboard appears. `--host-text`, `--host-return-search` and `--host-return-automatically` set the host field. The project generator now accepts several files per UI test target. Evidence and the remaining device checks are in the audit; [native-ci 40107374846](https://github.com/shomoD9/mind-machine/actions/runs/40107374846) passed every job with 13 of 13 keyboard UI tests.

## Markdown editor ticket 2: source-preserving live styles

Mac Capture uses one explicitly constructed TextKit 2 text view, built by `MacMarkdownEditor.makeScrollableEditor()` so the app and its benchmark share one configuration. Plain source carries only the body font and label colour, with no explicit paragraph style, matching a stock text view; list lines add their own indentation style. Its storage string remains the original Markdown source; `MacMarkdownEditor` supplies disposable attributes and the Mac adapter publishes source with no RTF encoding. iPhone and keyboard editing retain their existing behavior. Pasting into Mac Capture uses plain source; automatic quote/dash/text substitutions and automatic links are disabled. Copy and vault save read the literal source. Restoring a Mac draft ignores rich payloads and derives presentation again.

The dependency-free `MarkdownParagraph` in the portable core recognizes the ticket 2 subset: one to three opening hashes plus a space, `- `, `- [ ] ` / `- [x] `, asterisk emphasis and inline `[label](destination)` links. It returns UTF-16 spans without normalizing text. Headings use a larger font, lists/tasks use continuation indentation, bold/italic compose with heading fonts, and link labels are underlined. Syntax characters and link destinations remain visible in secondary ink. Inline code and lines beginning with a fence or code indentation stay plain. This bounded subset does not claim CommonMark completeness; cross-paragraph fence/reference/container invalidation is ticket 3.

The text-storage delegate records character edits, then the text-view delegate styles their containing paragraph after native character processing. Inserted/deleted newlines include the newly affected paragraphs. Only differing attributes are applied, outside undo registration. Selection/scroll remain native; marked text postpones presentation until composition commits. Prefix removal resets obsolete attributes. Paragraph discovery reads UTF-16 directly from text storage, stopping at an 8,192-unit affected-span cap before copying, parsing or enumerating attributes. Oversized paragraphs and bulk input keep native source attributes; later edits can style individual bounded paragraphs. Attribute transactions start only when a bounded style run actually differs, and unchanged typing attributes are left alone. There is no background parser, list continuation, command handling or marker hiding.

Portable parser tests cover prefixes, escaped/incomplete syntax, nested traits, Unicode ranges and bounded adversarial input.
